## A Historical and Theological Analysis of the Book of Judith

Judith is a book in the Bible's Old Testament. It's considered deuterocanonical, or of a second canon, because mainstream
Protestants and Jews do not consider Judith scripture, while mainstream Catholics and Orthodox Christians do. The reason
for this discrepancy is the book allegedly contains numerous historical inaccuracies that sacred scripture wouldn't
contain. In this blog post, I will first provide a very brief synopsis of the book's contents. Then, I will provide a
list of the alleged historical inaccuracies. Next, I will provide a list of proposed solutions to the alleged historical
inaccuracies and evaluate their merit. Finally, I will conclude with a theological analysis of the text.

---

### A Brief Synopsis

Judith begins in the twelfth year of the reign of king Nebuchadnezzar, who the text says reigns from Nineveh and is king
of the Assyrians (Jdt 1:1, 7). The initial conflict of the text is with another king, Arphaxad, who rules over the Medes
in Ecbatana (Jdt 1:1). The text doesn't specify a reason for this conflict, but the text spends several verses describing
how Arphaxad built walls to fortify Ecbatan (Jdt 1:2-4), so perhaps he was leading a rebellion against Nebuchadnezzar.
Either way, Nebuchadnezzar decides Arphaxad is a threat worth dealing with, so he wages war against him, with several
nearby nations presumably subservient to him coming to his aid (Jdt 1:6). Nebuchadnezzar, however, decides to call more
nations to battle, sending a message to all kingdoms in the ancient Near Eastern world that they should come fight with 
him in battle (Jdt 1:7-10). Much to Nebuchadnezzar's chagrin, the nations he sent messengers to all scoffed at his
message and refuse to assist him in his war with Arphaxad, causing him great indignation and in his wrath, he swears that
he will punish those rebellious kingdoms for their insolence (Jdt 1:11-12). But before he can do that, he has to deal
Arphaxad, which he does in the seventeenth year of his reign, quelling the rebellion, subduing the rowdy Medes, and 
returning back to Nineveh to celebrate.

A short time later, now in the eighteenth year of his reign, Nebuchadnezzar decides that he wants to make good on his 
promise to punish the nations he wrote that disobeyed him, regarding his conflict with Arphaxad, so he calls his chief
general, a man named Holofernes, and instructs him to destroy and subjugate all the western nations that disobeyed him
(Jdt 2:1-13). And Holofernes obliges, marching westward and destroying every nation in his path, until he comes to Judea
(Jdt 2:14-28; 3:1-10).

Note: Depending on the version of Judith read, the events differ slightly. In the Greek manuscripts, which are being 
referenced above, Nebuchadnezzar doesn't finish dealing with Arphaxad until the seventeenth year of his reign and sends
Holofernes on his westward conquest in the eighteenth year of his reign. In the Latin manuscripts, while the conflict
with Arphaxad still happens in the twelfth year of Nebuchadnezzar's reign, it also gets resolved that same year and he
sends Holofernes on his westward campaign in the thirteenth year of his reign. The Latin manuscripts also have some 
differences regarding the names of specific individuals, like Bethulia's elders or the high priest at the time, which
haven't been referenced above yet, and is a shorter manuscript overall. The [reasons for this](https://www.tertullian.org/fathers/jerome_preface_judith_e.htm) are attested to by the man who did the Latin 
translation, Jerome. In short, he states the authority of Judith was not universally accepted, but because it was written
in Aramaic, those at the Nicene Council considered it scripture, and because he was personally asked to translate it, he
decided to do so in one night. As a result, his translation isn't word-for-word, but conveys the overall meaning of the 
text, and when comparing the Aramaic manuscripts he had access to, there were inconsistencies he decided to not bother
translating. For the purposes of this article, all citations follow the Greek manuscripts and the text will align with
the King James Version.
